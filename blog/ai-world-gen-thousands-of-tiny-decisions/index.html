Post two · a project write-up

AI World Gen: thousands of tiny decisions, and a loop that graded them

A map generator that asks a model one question per tile. A new kind of model that answers in milliseconds for a fraction of a cent. And the evaluation loop that took it from a green blur to a village in eleven measured versions, most of them written by an agent.

Guillem Poy 20 September 2026 31 minute read

A model that decides instead of writes

AI World Gen started at the AI Hackathon Barcelona 2026. The idea: describe a place, and watch an AI build its map one tile at a time. The interesting question was never "can a model draw a map". It was: given what is around this tile, what goes here? That question is asked hundreds of times per map.

Asking a chat model each time is slow, expensive, and turns a one-word answer into text you have to parse. Jev, made by TypeSafe, is a different kind of model. You send it a state (a JSON object describing the situation) and a typed question: pick one of these options. It answers with a probability for every option. It does not write text at all. It is built for speed (TypeSafe quotes tens of milliseconds for the model itself) and it is priced at about $0.04 per million input tokens, roughly fifty times less than the chat models I would otherwise have used for the same call. The exact bill is a section of its own below.

So the generator splits the work in two. One creative call goes to a language model, once per world: it invents the vocabulary, the six to twenty-four kinds of tile the described map is made of, with their rules. Then one decision goes to Jev, once per tile, with the vocabulary as the options and the map so far as the state.

From there it is a loop, and the loop is the design. Code picks the next cell, builds the state around it, and asks Jev one question. The answer is written into the map, and that map is the state of the next question: the model deciding the fifty-seventh cell sees the fifty-six cells decided before it. When no cell is left, the map is finished.

What it costs, and how that grows

The bill for the whole project, from OpenRouter's usage page: $3.11 for 39.2 thousand decisions, and $0.33 for the 7 calls that wrote vocabularies. One decision costs eight thousandths of a cent; one vocabulary costs $0.047. Every map in this post, every version, every rerun: three dollars and change.

The total is not the interesting number, though. This is: what does one map cost, and what happens when the map gets bigger? The two ways of drawing it answer differently.

  • Jev, a decision per cell. Every question carries the state, and the state holds the map. A bigger map means more questions, and each question is longer.
  • An LLM, one call for the whole map. The prompt goes out once, and the model writes back about one letter per cell. A bigger map only means a longer answer.

The two cost the same at about nine tiles by nine. Under that size, a decision per cell is cheaper. Over it, the single call pulls ahead and never looks back: 8 times cheaper at 32 × 32, 16 times at 64 × 64.

Jev costs about fifty times less than a chat model per token, and the per-cell loop spends all of that on repetition. It is still the reason the loop is affordable at all: the same loop run with Claude Sonnet 5 instead of Jev would cost $1.22 for one 16 × 16 map.

Both ways pay for the vocabulary first, $0.047 for one creative call, once per world. For a small map, that is more than the drawing of it costs.

Version one was a green blur

The first version came out of an afternoon of vibe coding: describe a place, press the button, watch the tiles land one by one. It worked, in the sense that every call was answered and a map came out at the end. Then I looked at the map.

It was useless. Thirteen of the fifteen maps in the first test set used one tile type for more than 85% of the cells: a village that is all grass, a station that is all corridor.

The reason is obvious once you see it. A model that decides one cell from its neighbours, and whose neighbours are all grass, picks grass. Every correct local decision adds up to a wrong map.

I could have looked at three maps, changed the prompt, looked at three more, and felt good. I have done that before and I know how it ends: the change that fixes the village breaks the station. So before touching the generator, I built a way to measure it, and then a loop around that: change, measure, compare, change again, with every version scored on the same maps, so no improvement can quietly undo an older one.

Measuring instead of looking

The measurement lives in Galtea, an evaluation platform for AI products. I work there, so read this as a demonstration as much as a recommendation. Five words carry the whole thing, and they fit inside each other.

  • A specification is one sentence about what a good map does. “Every walkable area is reachable, and the map is playable.”
  • A metric turns that sentence into a number from 0 to 1. Almost every one is plain code in this project: “doors sit in walls” is a loop over the door tiles, “one walkable region” is a flood fill.
  • A test case is one seed: a preset, a grid size, a fill order and a random seed. The same 38 run against every version, so two versions are always compared on the same worlds.
  • A version is one change to the generator. Run it over the test cases and each map becomes a session, kept with its scores.
  • A judge answers the one question code cannot: does this look like a village? A model reads the finished map and scores it.

The specifications grew as the failures showed up. Three at the start: barriers form structures, paths form routes, everything walkable is reachable. A fourth after version 4, when the maps stopped being debris and started being wrong in a subtler way: a place reads as a place. Three more after version 7: the vocabulary's own rules hold, routes lead to doors and off the map, and the landmarks are there once without flooding the map.

Version 8: the code draws the rooms

Version 8 is worth its own stop, because it is the version that takes work away from the model.

Seven versions tried to get rooms out of the prompt: more context, the whole map written out as text, softer sampling, typed placement rules. Doors kept landing in open ground. A model that answers one cell at a time cannot draw a rectangle. It never sees the shape it is in the middle of, so it closes a wall line into a solid block, and it puts the door wherever the neighbours allow one.

So version 8 stops asking. Before the first cell is decided, code draws a plan of the map. The plan is geometry, and there is no model in it.

  • The vocabulary declares its structures. A cottage is a ring of cottage wall around cottage floor, with one cottage door.
  • The code places each one as a rectangle, with a random size and count inside the limits the vocabulary declares, and empty cells between rectangles. Together they never take more than half the map.
  • Each rectangle gets one door. It sits in a wall, never in a corner, on the side that faces the middle of the map, so the door opens onto the map and not off its edge.
  • Every cell now knows its part: a wall, the door, the inside of a named structure, or outside every structure.

The model still decides every cell, but the question is narrower. It is no longer “what goes here”, it is “what goes in this wall”, and the answer can be a window. An inside cell can be a floor or a chest. An outside cell can be anything that belongs outdoors. The plan is drawn from the seed of the test case, so the same test case gets the same plan every time.

Nothing else in the table moved a specification this far: doors in walls went from 0.15 to 0.96, every map got a room you can enter, and “a place reads as a place” went from 0.52 to 0.89. The price was the vocabulary. Coverage fell to 0.52, because a cell inside a room is never offered the types that belong outside it.

The lesson carries past this project. Give the model the decisions that are judgements: what stands in this room, who walks here, what this place is for. Give the code the decisions that are geometry.

Regressions, and what the loop caught

Two things I would not have learnt from looking at pictures.

A new metric rewrites history. Version 1 scored 0.99 on “paths form routes”. That metric asks whether the path cells join up, and in a map that is all corridor they do: there is no lone path cell to find. A second metric arrived later and asked how much of the map the paths take. Version 1 fell to 0.74.

A version table is only comparable if every row is scored with the same rules. So every new metric was run back over every old map, and every new test seed was drawn again with the code of each old version. Read the charts knowing that: the scores are the rules of today applied to the maps of yesterday.

A fix in one place lowers a score in another. Version 6 raised the sampling floor, so the generator takes the best answer of the model more often, and a long shot less often. Open ground stopped being speckled with odd single tiles, and ground in patches went from 0.25 to 0.52. The same change meant the rare types were almost never picked, and the share of the vocabulary that reaches the map fell from 0.72 to 0.54. One mansion seed carries both halves.

Version 11 made the same trade somewhere else. It gave the space station an open deck to stand on, which cut the corridor from half the map to a tenth of it. What is left of the corridors are short stubs, so path continuity fell from 0.73 to 0.58 in the run that raised path share from 0.65 to 0.84.

Neither of these was visible in the one map I would have looked at. Both were visible in the mean over 38.

The same maps, one call each

That is the loop, and that is where it stopped. Eleven versions, each one change, each one measured. What follows is not a twelfth: it is the two experiments whose dashed lines have been crossing the charts all along. Each answers a question the loop could not, and each is told here as I first ran it, with version 11’s code. A section after them runs both again with the code of earlier versions.

The first is the one the cost chart raised. It said the whole map in one call gets cheaper the bigger the map is. Cheap is not the same as good, so I built it as a second mode of the generator and ran it through the same evaluation, as version v11-llm. Same 38 seeds, same shipped vocabularies, same blueprint. The prompt gives Claude Sonnet 5 what the per-cell loop gives Jev, written once: every type with its rules and its target share, each planned room with its coordinates and the types its walls, door and inside allow, and the rules a good map follows. The model answers with the grid as rows of letters; a wrong shape or an unknown letter goes back to it with the list of problems, three attempts at most.

The first real call taught me something before it drew anything. Left with its default settings, the model spent its entire 6,000-token budget thinking about an 8 by 8 village and returned an empty string: 68 seconds, $0.064, nothing to parse. With thinking turned off, the same call took under four seconds and half a cent. Every number below is with thinking off.

On the map itself the single call loses, and it loses where I least expected. The plan is in its prompt room by room, with coordinates, and still only 47% of its maps hold an enclosed room, against 92% for the per-cell loop, and 60% of its doors sit in a wall against 87%. Writing 256 letters in one breath, the model drifts: a wall one column off, a door in the grass, a room that never closes. The per-cell loop cannot drift, because the blueprint is applied to each cell before the model is asked. The same holds for the typed rules, 0.76 against 0.99: Jev is never offered a type its rules forbid, and a text model breaks a few every map. Routes fell the most, 0.82 to 0.61, because its doors open onto nothing more often. It won on one specification, landmarks and story, 0.83 to 0.90: a model that sees the whole map places the one well and the one gate, where the per-cell loop still forgets them on half the maps (0.45 to 0.76). The judge, which reads the finished map and not the metrics, gives 0.65 against 0.69.

On time it wins outright: 3.7 seconds instead of 22 for an 8 by 8 map, 6 instead of 90 for 16 by 16, because 256 round trips become one. On money it is a tie: the whole run cost $0.25 in one call against $0.24 with Jev. The small map is cheaper with Jev ($0.005 against $0.006), because a 2,000-token prompt sent once costs more than 64 tiny decisions; the large map is cheaper in one call ($0.010 against $0.024), because the prompt does not grow with the map while the number of decisions does. Over the run, 46 calls did the work of 3,008, and 8 of the 38 maps needed a second answer because the first had the wrong number of rows or letters.

So which one? For a finished small map, fast, and a story that mentions every landmark, one call is the better tool, and it is now a switch in AI Setup. For rooms that close, doors in walls, rules that cannot be broken because the option was never offered, a map that fills in front of the reader, and the ability to ask again about one tile, the per-cell loop is the only one of the two that delivers. The honest summary is that the per-cell design was never justified by price; the two cost the same. It is justified by control, cell by cell, and the price of Jev is what makes that control affordable.

The same decisions, one request

One call for the whole map is fast because it is one request, not because a language model is better at this job. It also throws the typed decision away, which is the part of the project I care about. There is a third way, and it had been sitting in the request shape the whole time. The decisions endpoint takes questions as a map of named questions, and nothing in it says that map holds one entry. So I kept every per-cell question exactly as it was, and sent them together. An 8 by 8 map stops being 64 requests and becomes one request carrying 64 questions.

This is an experiment, not a new default. I wanted to know three things: whether Jev would answer that many independent questions at once, what it would cost, and what it would do to the map.

  • Every question keeps what it had. The same code builds it. The world, the blueprint, the cell’s part of the plan, the options narrowed to that part, the balance sheet, the whole map as one letter per cell: all of it is what the loop already sends Jev, cell by cell.
  • No question can have the other cells of its own batch. They are answered in the same request, so they do not exist yet when the question is written. Every question in a batch reads the same map, and that map has a hole in it exactly where the batch is.
  • So the rules that read neighbours go with them. “Never next to another portrait”, “exactly one staircase”, the cap on an over-used ground, the hint that continues a line four cells long: every one of those is worked out from the map as it stands when the batch is built. Inside a batch they are worked out from nothing.

Two things the first requests taught me before they drew a single map. There is a ceiling: 128 cell questions, 60,023 input tokens, answered in 1.2 seconds; 160 questions, about 71,600 tokens, refused outright with max_tokens_exceeded. So the limit is around 65,536 input tokens, and it is a token limit, not a question limit. And most of those tokens were my own words repeated. The instruction that tells Jev how to choose is about 1,200 characters and it is the same sentence for every cell; sent once per question it was 23,000 of the 56,000 input tokens of an 8 by 8 request. Moving it into the shared state, the same words sent once, dropped that map to 39,000 tokens, which is how 64 questions came to fit in one request at all.

A 16 by 16 map does not fit: 256 questions is about 177,000 input tokens, so it takes four or five requests. Rather than pretend otherwise I let it take them, in the test case’s own generation order, and let each later batch see what the earlier ones placed. That is the most information batching allows, and it means the three large maps are a partly batched, partly sequential hybrid. The 35 small maps are the clean case: 34 of them are one request each.

Then I ran the same evaluation on it, as version v11-batch: same 38 seeds, same shipped vocabularies, same blueprint, same orders, same metrics, same judge. Jev answered every one of the 3,008 questions. Nothing fell back, and nothing came back malformed.

The map is where it is decided, and here it is one map, the same 16 by 16 mansion from the same seed, drawn both ways.

Thirty-nine staircases is not a bug in the code, and it is not Jev misreading the question. Each of those cells was asked, on its own, “this is an interior cell of a mansion, and the world has no staircase yet: what goes here?”, and each one gave the same sensible answer. In the loop the second cell is never offered the staircase, because by then the first one has it. In a batch there is no first one. The same mechanism runs through every number that fell:

  • Cells contradict each other, and only each other. Across the run, 181 pairs of neighbours break a “never next to” rule, and all 181 are pairs decided in the same batch. The per-cell loop breaks 11 in the same 38 maps. The typed rule still works; there is simply nothing to apply it to yet.
  • One-of-a-kind things arrive in crowds. Seven of the 38 maps hold a type whose rules say “exactly one” more than once, against none in the loop. “Landmarks, once” fell from 1.00 to 0.67.
  • Lines stop being lines. A path continues because the cell before it was a path. With nothing before it, 0.92 of path cells touching another path becomes 0.46, continuity 0.58 becomes 0.26, and the share of maps whose main route reaches the edge 0.79 becomes 0.40.
  • The map gets emptier. Identical states give similar answers, so the common type wins nearly everywhere: vocabulary coverage 0.77 to 0.43, landmarks present 0.45 to 0.25. The village below has its two cottages and their doors, and no villager, no well, no market stall and no path.

What did not move is as interesting. Everything the code decides survived the change intact, because the code decides it per cell either way, before the model is asked: structures 0.98 to 0.97, cells in the right zone 1.00, doors set into a wall 0.87 up to 0.94, a room you can enter on 0.95 of maps. Two things even improved, for an unflattering reason: ground comes in patches 0.65 to 0.83, and one single walkable region 0.72 to 0.93. A map that repeats itself is a tidy map. It is tidy and it is empty.

Now the part worth being careful about, because four different things get called “cheaper” and they are not the same thing.

Sixty-one times fewer requests buys 26 times less waiting and 2.8 times less money. The three numbers are different because they are three different things. Latency collapses because the round trips collapse: one wait instead of 64. Cost falls much less, because you pay for tokens, not for requests, and the tokens did not fall nearly as far: 6.0 million down to 2.04 million. The saving is real but it is narrow, and it is entirely the shared state. The world, the blueprint, the balance sheet and the map sketch used to be re-sent with every single cell; now they are sent once per batch. What each question still carries on its own, its cell and its own list of options with their rules, is most of the payload, and 64 of those cost 64 of those whether they travel together or apart.

So: yes, Jev will answer a map’s worth of independent typed questions in one request, at a tenth of the latency and a third of the price, and it answers all of them. And on these 38 seeds, with this generator, the maps are worse in the specific way the design predicts: every judgement that needed a neighbour lost the neighbour. The judge, reading the finished maps, puts it at 0.57 against 0.69.

That is one experiment on one generator, and it is not a verdict on batching. What it does say is where the line falls. The blueprint is code, applied per cell, and it did not care at all. The rules that read the map are code too, but they can only read what is there, and in a batch nothing is. If I wanted batching to work here, the fix would not be a better prompt: it would be to move more of what the cells currently tell each other into the plan that is drawn before any of them are asked. Which is the same lesson version 8 taught, arriving from the other direction.

The two shortcuts, at every stage

Both experiments above ran with version 11’s code: its vocabularies, its rules, its blueprint. That leaves a question neither section can answer. Did the shortcuts get better along with the loop, or would they have been this good at version 1? So I ran each of them again with the code of four earlier versions: v1, before any quality work; v4, the lines; v7, the typed rules; v8, the blueprint. They are the dashed lines in the charts at the top, five points each, and the last two columns of the table of maps.

  • Each run knows what its version knew, and no more. The code is checked out at the version’s own commit. A batch sends that version’s own per-cell questions, built by its own code, and what every cell of a batch shares is sent once. The one call gets the version’s vocabulary, target shares from v2 on, the plan from v8 on, and only the rules for a good map that the version had given Jev, in words or in code. At v11 that prompt is the v11 prompt to the character, and a test pins it.
  • One rule is in every prompt: every walkable cell must be reachable. No version of the loop told Jev that, because a single cell cannot see it. The v11 prompt asks for it, so every version’s does.
  • The batches meet the same ceiling. Before v8 every cell is offered every type, so each question is bigger, and an 8 by 8 map takes two requests: 54 cells, then 10. From v8 on a cell is offered only the types of its part of the plan, and all 64 fit in one.
  • Everything else is the same: the 38 seeds, the orders, the metrics and the judge. Each map is scored against its own version’s vocabulary, the way the loop’s older versions were. Versions 1 to 6 shipped no typed rules, so “the rules hold” has nothing to check on their shortcuts and is left blank.

How big must a difference be before it means anything? I ran the new batch code at v11 and set it against the v11-batch run above: the same questions, packed a second way and drawn a second time. Every specification landed within 0.05 of the first run, some higher and some lower. Each point below is one run, so I read nothing into a gap smaller than that.

The one call was good from the start. With version 1’s knowledge, no shares, no plan and four sentences of rules, the judge gives its maps 0.68. With version 11’s it gives 0.65. The loop first got there at version 9, one version after the blueprint: 0.09 at version 1, 0.69 at version 9. Much of what the loop spent twenty hours learning, the single call did on its first try: every type on the map (coverage 0.83 at v1-llm, against 0.15 for version 1), no type flooding it, the landmarks placed. Those are exactly the things a model that writes the whole map at once can see, and a model that decides one cell cannot.

Two changes moved the one call by more than the noise, and both are rules about where things go. The typed rules at v7 took routes to doors from 0.51 to 0.65. The plan at v8 took “a place reads as a place” from 0.62 to 0.78, and a room closes on 74% of its maps against 34% a version earlier. The plan also cost it a little: reachable 0.90 to 0.82, landmarks 0.90 to 0.84. The judge did not move at all: 0.68, 0.68, 0.68, 0.65, 0.65. And v11 gave part of the rooms back, closing one on 47% of its maps. I cannot tell from these runs why; the v8 and v11 prompts differ by two rules and by the station and city vocabularies.

The batched run is the other way round. It never came close to the loop before version 8, and then it jumped at one version. The judge reads 0.20 at v1, 0.32 at v4, 0.25 at v7, then 0.57 at v8 and 0.57 at v11. The reason is the one the section above gave, now measured at every stage. Versions 2 to 7 all work through the cells decided before: the balance sheet counts them, the hints continue their lines, the sketch shows them, the typed rules check them. A batch has none of those cells, so each of those fixes arrives with nothing to work on. Paths stay between 0.55 and 0.61, and at v7 not one door stands in a wall. The blueprint is different. It is code, applied to each cell before the question is asked, so a batch gets all of it at once: structures from 0.64 to 0.98, and “a place reads as a place” from 0.39 to 0.87.

There is one place where the batch beats the loop, and it is the first version. Version 1 failed by copying its neighbours: a village that is all grass, a station that is all corridor. A batch has no neighbours to copy, so v1-batch floods less (no type dominates 0.42, against 0.14) and the judge gives it 0.20 against 0.09. That is the loop’s worst failure taken away, not a good map.

The price moved with the ceiling. Before v8 a batched run of the 38 maps took 86 or 87 requests and $0.11 to $0.12; from v8, 46 to 49 requests and $0.08 to $0.09. The one call cost $0.21 to $0.31 a run at every stage.

So the question has two answers. The one call was about as good at version 1 as at version 11, and of everything the loop learned, only the rules about where things go reached it. The batch got better alongside the loop only where a fix was code applied to each cell, and not at all where a fix needed the cell next door. These are five runs of each, on one generator and 38 seeds, and they are measurements, not laws.

Running the loop with an agent

Most of the eleven versions were written by an agent, Claude Code, working in a loop I set up and then mostly watched. One turn looked like this. Read the previous version's scores and the pictures where it lost. Write down one theory ("the model does not see enough of the map", "the sampling is adding noise", "the rules are prose, so they are advice"). Change the code with tests. Commit, so the version in Galtea records exactly what was measured. Run the 38 seeds. Compare. Write the result into the project's README, in one paragraph per version, so the next turn starts from the memory of this one.

My part was smaller and, I think, the part that mattered. I said which theory to test first. When I saw a failure the numbers missed, I asked for a specification for it, which is how four rules became seven. I set the budget: 8 by 8 grids for the bulk of the seeds, and only three 16 by 16 maps, because a room needs space to exist but a large map costs eight times a small one. And I said when to stop: when the agent could no longer name a change it was confident would move a number, the loop had done its work for now.

The agent also broke things. It once committed a version with a failing test, because a shell pipe hid the exit code. It once ran two evaluations against modules it was editing at the same time. Both were caught, one by the continuous integration check and one by the maps coming out wrong, and both became a written rule for the next turn. The loop is not a promise that every version is better. It is a promise that you will know.

What I have not tried: decisions that branch

Both ways of using Jev in this post are flat. The world has one layer of children, and every child is a cell:

world → cell, cell, cell, cell, … 3,008 times.

Every question is the same size and the same shape, and they either go one at a time or all at once. There is a third shape I have not built, where the decisions are a tree instead of a list, and each layer asks about what the layer above it decided:

world → regions → structures → rooms → objects.

  • Decide the broad areas first. This corner is the docks, that one is the old quarter, this strip between them is farmland. A handful of typed decisions for the whole map.
  • Then, for each area, decide what stands in it. The docks get three warehouses and a crane; the old quarter gets a chapel and six houses. The question is asked once per area, and the options depend on which area it is.
  • Then, for each structure, decide its inside. A warehouse is one long hall; a house is a kitchen and two rooms.
  • Then, for each room, decide what is in it. Only here does the question get down to the size of a tile, and by then it is asked about a room that already knows what it is for.

The point is not that there are fewer decisions. It is that the later ones depend on the earlier ones by construction, rather than by hoping the model reads a map sketch and works it out. A cell in the kitchen of a house in the old quarter is a much narrower question than a cell at (7, 12), and the narrowing was itself decided, not hard-coded. That is also what this project keeps discovering from the other direction: the blueprint in version 8 is exactly one layer of such a tree, written by hand in code, and it moved more numbers than any prompt change did.

It could suit a decision model particularly well, because each step is still a small typed choice with a handful of options, which is what these models are fast and cheap at, while the structure and the dependencies live in the shape of the tree rather than inside one long prompt. It may also fail for reasons I cannot see from here: an early wrong branch would take a whole district with it, and the flat design at least fails one cell at a time.

I have not built it and I have not measured it. There are no numbers for it in this post, nothing in the version table, and nothing in Galtea. It is the next experiment I want to run, and until it has been through the same 38 seeds it is only a plausible-sounding idea, which this project has taught me to distrust.

What is still wrong

The corridors are stubs: a route between two doors is a shape, and shapes are the plan's job, so the next version should draw the paths the way it draws the rooms. Rooms are often empty, because the state says what the world lacks but not what a kitchen holds. Free-standing walls still split some maps in two. And rooms never share a wall, which real streets do all the time. Each of those has a number now, which is the point.
